Declaration of Adoption

This Disaster Risk Reduction and Resilience Implementation Plan for Ga-Pila (Sterkwater) (2025–2030) has been developed in accordance with the Disaster Management Act 57 of 2002, the National Disaster Management Framework (2005), and the principles of cooperative governance. The Plan reflects the specific risk conditions, vulnerabilities, capacities and development priorities of the Ga-Pila community and is informed by participatory risk assessment, structured community consultation, and sound disaster risk management logic.

This Plan is authentic and community-specific to Ga-Pila (Sterkwater). It articulates local disaster risk priorities grounded in lived experience, vulnerability analysis, land-use realities and existing community resilience mechanisms. All information contained herein has been validated through community engagement processes, local institutional inputs, and field-based hazard and risk assessments.

The Plan is designed as a practical, working implementation framework rather than a static policy document. It will be reviewed annually to ensure continued relevance to changing risk conditions, settlement dynamics, development patterns and community priorities. The Ga-Pila Community Development Trust is designated as the official custodian and coordination authority of this Plan on behalf of the community, with responsibility for oversight, alignment and facilitation of implementation.

We, the undersigned, hereby adopt the Ga-Pila Disaster Risk Reduction and Resilience Implementation Plan (2025–2030) as the official disaster risk management framework for Ga-Pila (Sterkwater). We endorse this Plan as a community-led, development-centred approach that reduces disaster risk, safeguards lives, livelihoods and community assets, and strengthens long-term resilience. We commit to its implementation, regular review and continuous improvement in the collective interest of present and future generations.

Foreword

Disaster risk in Ga-Pila (Sterkwater) is neither abstract nor occasional. It is experienced in the disruption of daily life, in damage to homes and infrastructure, in threats to water security, in fire incidents, in road accidents, and in the gradual pressures created by poverty, land constraints and environmental change. These risks affect not only safety but also dignity, livelihoods and the long-term development of the community. Climate variability and changing settlement patterns are increasing both the frequency and intensity of these challenges, placing additional strain on households and local systems.

This Disaster Risk Management Plan is a response to that reality. It has been developed to provide Ga-Pila with a clear, practical and community driven framework for reducing risk, strengthening preparedness and improving coordinated response. The Plan recognises that while external assistance is important, the first line of protection lies within the community itself. Households, local institutions, traditional leadership and volunteers play a decisive role in anticipating hazards, reducing exposure and responding effectively when incidents occur.

The Plan positions disaster risk management as an integral part of development rather than a separate emergency function. It emphasises prevention, preparedness and resilience building, alongside response and recovery. It also introduces clear coordination arrangements, defines roles and responsibilities, and strengthens linkages between the community, ward structures and municipal and district disaster management systems. In doing so, it seeks to move Ga-Pila away from reactive crisis management towards organised, predictable and accountable risk management.

Central to this approach is local leadership. The Ga-Pila Community Development Trust serves as the custodian and coordination authority of this Plan, working in collaboration with the Ga-Pila Traditional Council, the Disaster Management Volunteer Force, ward structures and key service institutions. This leadership model ensures that disaster risk management is locally anchored, transparent and aligned with broader community development priorities. Support from government and partners is welcomed, but it must reinforce, rather than bypass, community systems and decision-making processes.

This Plan is not intended to remain static. It is a living framework that will be reviewed and refined as risk conditions, settlement patterns and development priorities evolve. Its effectiveness will depend on consistent implementation, shared responsibility and sustained commitment from all role players. Through this Plan, Ga-Pila affirms its determination to protect life, safeguard livelihoods and strengthen resilience for present and future generations.

Together, we will build a resilient Ga-Pila.

Introduction 

Background

Bellevue Farm, although spatially separate from the village and located approximately 20 kilometres from the Ga-Pila residential settlement, is a community-owned productive asset comprising Portion 2 and Portion 5 of Farm Bellevue, with a combined extent of approximately 656 hectares. The Farm is held and operated under the custodianship of the Ga-Pila Community Development Trust for the benefit of the community.

As a landholding of this scale, Bellevue Farm plays a significant role in livelihoods, employment creation, food security, environmental stewardship and long-term community development. Disaster risks affecting the Farm include drought, fire, flooding, land degradation, infrastructure failure, occupational safety incidents, theft and damage to productive assets. While these risks may not always present as immediate residential emergencies, their impacts extend directly to the Ga-Pila community through loss of income, disruption of livelihoods, asset erosion and reduced development capacity.

The physical separation between the village and a 656-hectare operational farm introduces additional disaster risk considerations, including delayed response times, communication challenges and coordination complexity during emergencies. At the same time, shared ownership and Trust custodianship mean that risk management on Bellevue Farm cannot be separated from community disaster risk management. Effective resilience for Ga-Pila therefore depends on protecting both households and this extensive land-based asset under a unified disaster risk management framework.

Purpose of the Plan

Purpose

The purpose of the Ga-Pila (Sterkwater) Disaster Risk Management Plan is to establish a structured and coordinated approach to disaster risk reduction, preparedness, response and recovery across the Ga-Pila residential community and Bellevue Farm, comprising Portion 2 and Portion 5 of Farm Bellevue, a 656-hectare community-owned landholding located approximately 20 kilometres from the village and operated under the custodianship of the Ga-Pila Community Development Trust.

The Plan formalises disaster risk management arrangements at community level and ensures that risks affecting households, social infrastructure, productive land and community-owned assets are addressed within a single, coherent framework. It provides practical guidance for managing hazards in both proximate residential settings and remote operational environments, while strengthening coordination, accountability and alignment with broader community development and asset management priorities.

Objectives

The objectives of the Ga-Pila (Sterkwater) Disaster Risk Management Plan are to:

reduce disaster risk to people, livelihoods, infrastructure, land and community-owned assets across both the Ga-Pila residential settlement and Bellevue Farm;

identify, assess and prioritise hazards, vulnerabilities and exposure affecting households, social facilities, productive land and operational activities;

strengthen prevention and mitigation measures that reduce the likelihood and impact of disasters in residential and land-based contexts;

establish preparedness and early warning arrangements that take into account the physical separation between the village and Bellevue Farm;

define clear coordination, communication and response procedures for incidents occurring within the settlement and on remote landholdings;

support effective recovery, rehabilitation and continuity of livelihoods and operations following disaster events;

strengthen alignment between community governance structures, Trust custodianship arrangements and municipal and district disaster management systems; and

promote shared responsibility for disaster risk management as a foundation for long-term resilience and sustainable development.

These objectives ensure that DRM becomes a community-owned, structured and sustainable system rather than a reactive activity.

The Plan further serves as the primary guiding framework for disaster risk management activities undertaken in collaboration with ward structures, the local municipality and the District Disaster Management Centre.

Community Profile

Introduction

This chapter provides a detailed profile of the physical, social, economic and institutional contexts within which disaster risk manifests in Ga-Pila. It establishes an evidence-based understanding of exposure, vulnerability and capacity, which is essential for informed hazard analysis and the design of appropriate disaster risk reduction, preparedness and response measures.

The chapter is deliberately structured around the two distinct but interrelated geographic and functional spaces covered by this Disaster Risk Management Plan. Part A focuses on the Ga-Pila residential community (Sterkwater) as a settlement and social environment, where disaster risks directly affect households, services and everyday life. Part B, which follows, profiles Bellevue Farm, comprising Portion 2 and Portion 5 of Farm Bellevue, as a community-owned land and productive asset under the custodianship of the Ga-Pila Community Development Trust.

This dual structure recognises that disaster risk in Ga-Pila does not arise only within the residential settlement, nor only within land-based operations, but across both spaces through different mechanisms and with different consequences. Profiling these contexts separately, yet within a single chapter, ensures that subsequent risk analysis and planning respond to the full spatial, social and economic footprint of the community.

Part A: Ga-Pila Community (Sterkwater)

Overview of Ga-Pila 

Ga-Pila, also known as Sterkwater, is a rural settlement located within Mogalakwena Local Municipality in the Waterberg District Municipality of Limpopo Province. The community functions as the primary residential and social space for its residents, accommodating households, public facilities, informal and formal economic activity, and key governance and coordination structures.

Data informing this profile was collected through a structured household survey process undertaken during the development of the Ga-Pila Integrated Community Development Plan. Local enumerators collected data using electronic tools, covering approximately 400 households per week, which provides a credible statistical basis for analysing vulnerability, exposure and coping capacity at community level.

From a disaster risk management perspective, Ga-Pila is characterised by recurring, low- to medium-intensity risks rather than infrequent catastrophic events. These risks accumulate over time through service disruptions, environmental exposure and socio-economic stress, steadily eroding household resilience.

Geographic Location and Administrative Context

Ga-Pila is situated within a predominantly rural landscape under traditional authority. The settlement pattern is largely low-density and horizontally spread, reflecting incremental growth over time. Residential stands are organised into distinct sections of the village, with varying access to services and infrastructure.

The main road from R518 is tarred, however internal village roads are gravel and are vulnerable to erosion and access disruption during periods of heavy rainfall. Limited formal stormwater management increases the risk of runoff damage and temporary isolation of certain areas, particularly those located on lower ground or further from main access routes. These conditions directly affect emergency response times and evacuation feasibility. 

Population Profile and Social Characteristics

Women constitute 52 percent of the Ga-Pila population. Household composition analysis shows that 64 percent of households consist of one to four persons, while 14 percent consist of five persons. Single-person households account for 19 percent of all households and are predominantly elderly and female-headed.

Housing, Infrastructure and Basic Services

Housing in Ga-Pila is predominantly formal, reflecting the gradual consolidation of the settlement over time. While most dwellings are permanent structures, assessments referenced in the Integrated Community Development Plan identify variability in construction quality, including cracked slabs, foundation erosion and settlement-related structural stress. These conditions increase vulnerability to damage during periods of heavy or prolonged rainfall.

Settlement density has increased in certain sections of the village due to the addition of backyard rooms and informal extensions. Although the ICDP does not quantify backyard structures as a discrete indicator, field observations confirm uneven densification. Reduced spacing between dwellings affects fire spread dynamics, limits the safe placement and operation of on-site sanitation facilities and constrains access for emergency vehicles.

Electricity access is widespread within Ga-Pila; however, supply reliability is affected by feeder capacity constraints and load shedding. During electricity interruptions, households rely on alternative energy sources such as firewood, candles and gas. This practice increases the likelihood of household fires, particularly in denser sections of the settlement and where informal electrical connections are present.

Sanitation access represents a significant structural vulnerability. ICDP data indicates that 55% of households use pit latrines, 22% use ventilated improved pit latrines, 11% use dry toilets, and 11% of households have no access to sanitation facilities.

Overall, 88% of households rely on on-site sanitation systems, which are sensitive to rainfall intensity, soil conditions and groundwater levels. Housing conditions, sanitation coverage and energy reliability interact to produce recurrent, low- to medium-intensity incidents that accumulate over time and erode household assets, health and coping capacity.

Water Access and Public Health Risk

Water access in Ga-Pila is uneven and constitutes a key determinant of household vulnerability. Data from the Integrated Community Development Plan indicates that 11% of households have no access to water infrastructure, relying on communal sources, neighbouring households or informal arrangements. This condition represents a persistent vulnerability under normal circumstances and escalates into a critical risk during periods of drought, infrastructure failure or emergency conditions.

Distance-to-water analysis shows that 15% of households travel distances between 500 metres and 1 kilometre to access water, while 3% of households travel distances exceeding 1 kilometre. These access patterns reduce household flexibility, increase physical strain and affect the ability to respond to concurrent risks, particularly for households with elderly members, children or limited mobility.

Water access constraints intersect directly with the sanitation profile described in Section 2.5. Limited water availability undermines hygiene practices and increases the likelihood of contamination, particularly during heavy rainfall events when runoff and rising groundwater levels occur.

Public health data referenced in the ICDP records recurring diarrhoeal disease incidents within the municipality associated with water quality and sanitation conditions. Roads, Stormwater and Access Risk

Road and stormwater infrastructure conditions in Ga-Pila play a critical role in shaping access, mobility and emergency response capacity. Municipal infrastructure data referenced in the Integrated Community Development Plan indicates that only 14.5% of roads within the municipality are surfaced, with the remainder comprising gravel or earth roads. Ga-Pila’s internal road network reflects this broader pattern, with most access routes remaining unsurfaced.

Gravel roads within the settlement are vulnerable to erosion, surface degradation and loss of passability during periods of heavy rainfall. Poorly defined road shoulders and limited drainage infrastructure result in runoff concentrating along road alignments, accelerating surface damage and forming ruts and gullies. These conditions reduce vehicle accessibility and increase travel times under normal conditions, with more severe impacts during adverse weather events.

Stormwater management within the village is largely informal. The absence of engineered drainage systems means that stormwater is channelled through natural flow paths and improvised roadside trenches. These channels are prone to blockage by sediment and debris, resulting in overtopping and the diversion of water into residential yards and access routes.

Community Facilities and Social Assets

Ga-Pila is served by a range of community facilities and social assets that support everyday social functioning and play an important role during emergencies. These include educational facilities, places of worship, community meeting spaces and administrative offices. The distribution and condition of these facilities influence both preparedness and response capacity within the community.

A central facility within the village is the Ga-Pila Community Hall complex, which has been repaired and refurbished and is currently fully functional. The complex comprises a main community hall, a designated study area and surrounding grounds suitable for gatherings and organised activities. Two offices within the facility are currently occupied, supporting community administration and coordination functions.

The kitchen component of the community hall is leased and used for other purposes and is presently congested with furniture and stored documents. While this does not affect the day-to-day use of the hall and study facilities, it limits the functionality of the complex during emergencies, particularly its potential use for catering, relief coordination or temporary shelter support that requires food preparation and storage capacity.

Educational facilities within Ga-Pila, including schools and early childhood development centres, constitute important social infrastructure. During emergencies, these facilities may be used informally as information points or temporary support spaces, although they are not formally designated or equipped as disaster shelters. Their structural condition, accessibility and proximity to vulnerable households are therefore relevant considerations in disaster preparedness planning.

Social assets in Ga-Pila extend beyond physical infrastructure. Faith-based organisations, burial societies, community volunteer groups and informal mutual-aid networks form a strong social fabric that supports households during times of stress. These structures provide assistance with caregiving, information dissemination and mobilisation of community support during incidents such as illness, bereavement or service disruptions.

While these social assets contribute significantly to coping capacity, their role in disaster response remains largely informal. There are limited formal linkages between these groups and municipal disaster management structures, and their potential contribution to organised preparedness, early warning dissemination and coordinated response is not yet fully institutionalised.

Physical and Environmental Context of Ga-Pila (Sterkwater)

Ga-Pila (Sterkwater) is situated within the Savanna biome of Limpopo Province, characterised by semi-arid climatic conditions, variable rainfall patterns and vegetation dominated by bushveld species. The community lies within the broader Mogalakwena landscape, where natural drainage systems, soil characteristics and climate variability play a decisive role in shaping environmental exposure and disaster risk.

The local climate is defined by summer rainfall occurring primarily between November and March, often delivered through short-duration, high-intensity thunderstorms. Rainfall variability is pronounced, with extended dry periods punctuated by sudden storm events. These patterns influence surface runoff, soil stability and water availability, particularly in areas where natural drainage paths have been altered over time. Prolonged dry seasons, coupled with rising average temperatures, contribute to heat stress, dust exposure and increasing pressure on limited water resources.

Soils in and around Ga-Pila are predominantly sandy to sandy-clay in composition, with shallow depth in certain areas. These soil conditions are highly susceptible to erosion when exposed to concentrated runoff and are less capable of supporting stable foundations where drainage is poorly managed. During heavy rainfall events, surface water moves rapidly across compacted ground, forming informal flow paths that contribute to yard flooding, road degradation and sediment accumulation around homesteads.

Vegetation cover within the village and its immediate surroundings is uneven and, in places, degraded. Limited grass cover reduces the land’s capacity to absorb rainfall, increasing the speed and volume of runoff during storms. During dry periods, exposed surfaces generate dust, affecting air quality and compounding respiratory stress, particularly among children and older persons.

Ga-Pila is located within a high lightning frequency zone, consistent with broader patterns observed across the Waterberg and Mogalakwena regions. Thunderstorm activity is frequently accompanied by lightning strikes, posing risks to people, livestock and structures, particularly where grounding, lightning protection and safe shelter practices are limited.

The environmental context of Ga-Pila is further influenced by surrounding mining activity within the Mogalakwena area. Heavy vehicle movement, altered landforms and compacted surfaces associated with mining operations affect natural drainage behaviour beyond the immediate footprint of the village. During dry seasons, dust emissions contribute to reduced air quality, while ground vibration and soil disturbance have been reported to exacerbate cracking and settlement stress in nearby residential structures.

Governance, Community Structures and Institutional Systems

Governance in Ga-Pila operates through a layered system comprising traditional authority, elected local government representation and community-based development structures. These institutions collectively shape decision making, development coordination, accountability and conflict resolution within the village. While governance structures are present and active, coordination between them is uneven, and institutional effectiveness is strongly dependent on community initiative rather than sustained external support. Decision making in the village is therefore characterised by active participation but fragmented authority. Leadership structures respond to issues as they arise, but there is no single, integrated governance platform that routinely aligns development priorities, service delivery concerns and disaster risk management planning.

Traditional Leadership

Ga-Pila falls under the jurisdiction of the Mapela Traditional Authority, with village-level governance exercised through the Ga-Pila Traditional Council under the authority of the Senior Traditional Leader. Traditional leadership plays a central role in land allocation, settlement administration, cultural affairs and dispute resolution. It remains the most consistent and visible governance structure in the village.

The Traditional Council oversees allocation of residential sites, confirmation of residency and issuance of local settlement records, including the document commonly referred to as the green file. It also convenes community meetings and serves as the primary point of engagement with external stakeholders, including government departments, mining companies and development partners.

While traditional leadership enjoys broad legitimacy, governance challenges exist. These include disputes related to land allocation, community concerns regarding the affordability of administrative documentation and delays when matters require escalation beyond village level. Despite these constraints, traditional leadership remains the backbone of governance in Ga-Pila and plays a stabilising role during periods of uncertainty or conflict.

Ward Representation and Municipal Governance

Ga-Pila is located within Ward 6 of Mogalakwena Local Municipality. Municipal representation is primarily exercised through the Ward Councillor and the Ward Committee, which function as intermediaries between the community and the municipality. The Ward Committee is responsible for conveying community concerns, service delivery issues and development priorities to municipal structures. In practice, this interface is weak. Feedback from municipal offices is often delayed, and follow-up on service delivery issues is inconsistent. Municipal officials seldom attend village meetings, and residents report limited visibility of the municipality within Ga-Pila. The Waterberg District Municipality, which holds disaster management oversight responsibilities, has minimal routine engagement in the village outside of specific incidents or formal reporting processes.

Sector departments such as Health, Education and Social Development engage sporadically, usually in response to specific cases or invitations. The absence of regular departmental outreach contributes to gaps in accountability and reinforces a perception of institutional distance between the community and government.

Community Organisations and Development Structures

Ga-Pila has a distinctive community-led development structure in the form of the Ga-Pila Community Development Trust (GCDT). The Trust plays a significant role in coordinating development initiatives, managing agreements linked to relocation commitments and engaging external stakeholders on behalf of the community. It is recognised by both traditional leadership and development partners as a legitimate community representative structure.

In addition to the GCDT, various project-specific committees emerge around issues such as housing, land disputes and service delivery. However, these structures are generally informal, lack legal standing and are not integrated into municipal planning forums. Youth groups, women’s organisations, sports committees and faith-based initiatives operate independently, without a shared planning or coordination framework.

While the GCDT provides leadership and continuity, it also carries high expectations from the community. In the absence of sustained government support, it is often expected to fill institutional gaps beyond its mandate and resources. This places strain on its capacity and highlights the need for stronger institutional backing and clearer role delineation.

Coordination, Participation and Institutional Gaps

There is no standing village-wide coordination platform that brings together traditional leadership, ward structures and development organisations on a regular basis. Coordination occurs on an issue-by-issue basis and depends on which structure initiates engagement. This results in parallel decision-making spaces rather than integrated planning.

Community participation levels are high, and meetings are generally well attended. However, decisions taken in meetings are not always implemented due to unclear responsibility allocation and lack of follow-up mechanisms. Municipal and sector department participation is irregular, shifting the burden of coordination to community structures without enforcement authority.

Despite these gaps, Ga-Pila has strong potential for improved coordination. Active leadership, high participation and existing organisational spaces provide a foundation for establishing a structured community development and disaster risk coordination forum in future.

Local Economy and Livelihood Systems

The local economy of Ga-Pila is characterised by limited formal employment, a narrow local business base and heavy reliance on informal and survivalist livelihood strategies. Economic conditions reflect both the legacy of relocation, which disrupted land-based livelihoods, and the village’s partial integration into the mining-driven economy of the Mapela region.

Household income is derived mainly from wage labour linked to mining and secondary services, informal trading within the village and transfer income through social grants and remittances. Employment opportunities are largely insecure and contract-based, limiting income stability. Most economic activity occurs outside the village, and limited local circulation of income constrains economic growth.

Limited informal economy plays a critical role in household survival. Activities include spaza shops, cooked food vending, construction teams, transport services, informal mechanics, welding, backyard rentals and small livestock keeping. While unstructured, these activities demonstrate adaptability and economic initiative, but operate without access to infrastructure, finance or formal support.

Rental accommodation has become an increasingly important livelihood strategy, contributing to household income while increasing settlement density. The absence of a formal business area, marketplace or enterprise support infrastructure limits economic expansion and keeps the local economy shallow.

Economic vulnerability directly influences disaster risk. Limited income reduces households’ ability to improve housing safety, maintain electrical connections, invest in water storage or recover after hazard events. Strengthening local livelihoods is therefore integral to reducing disaster vulnerability.

Social Dynamics, Cultural Norms and Community Values

Social life in Ga-Pila is shaped by shared history, cultural identity and collective memory of relocation. Community cohesion remains strong, supported by extended family structures, cultural practices and widespread participation in community activities. Sepedi is the dominant language, reinforcing social cohesion and shared identity. Extended families play a central role in caregiving, resource sharing and crisis response. Burial societies and savings groups provide informal financial protection, while churches offer counselling, welfare support and moral guidance. These networks constitute a critical layer of social resilience.

However, social cohesion is under pressure. Youth unemployment, limited recreation opportunities and perceptions of exclusion from decision making contribute to frustration and rising social tension. Substance abuse and petty crime are emerging concerns. While traditional norms continue to promote respect and collective responsibility, informal social control mechanisms are weakening. Despite these challenges, social resilience remains one of Ga-Pila’s strongest assets. Community members mobilise quickly during crises, but sustained social stability will require stronger youth inclusion, structured social programmes and accessible conflict resolution mechanisms.

Part B: Bellevue Farm

Overview of Bellevue Farm

Bellevue Bellevue Farm, comprising Portion 2 and Portion 5 of Farm Bellevue, is a community-owned agricultural asset held in custodianship by the Ga-Pila Community Development Trust (GCDT). The farm was purchased by Anglo American and transferred to the Trust in 2023 as part of the company’s long-standing commitment arising from the relocation of the Ga-Pila community from Old Ga-Pila nearly two decades ago. The transfer was framed as an act of restorative justice, intended to compensate for historical displacement and the loss of land-based livelihoods.

From a disaster risk management perspective, Bellevue Farm represents a complex risk environment shaped as much by institutional history and governance conditions as by physical and environmental factors. While the transfer of land addressed a compliance obligation associated with restitution, restorative justice remains substantively incomplete. The community received a commercial farming asset without accompanying operational equipment, start-up capital, skills transfer, or structured capacity building. After nearly twenty years without active farming, the Trust inherited responsibility for a functioning commercial property without the institutional readiness required to operate it safely, productively or sustainably.

Bellevue Farm is not a high-potential commercial agricultural property by conventional standards. Its viability depends less on the inherent land quality and more on infrastructure integrity, asset protection, operational management capacity and access to capital. The very genesis of the restorative justice process therefore constitutes the farm’s most significant underlying hazard: a complex commercial asset placed under community custodianship without the systems, tools or experience needed to manage operational, environmental and security risks. This foundational condition shapes all subsequent risk drivers examined in Part B of this Chapter.

Geographic Context and Location of Bellevue Farm

Bellevue Farm is located within Mogalakwena Local Municipality in the Waterberg District of Limpopo Province, approximately 20 kilometres from the Ga-Pila residential settlement and about 50 kilometres from Mokopane town, the nearest major commercial and service centre. Regional access is provided via the R518 provincial road, a key transport corridor linking rural settlements, mining areas and agricultural zones within the district. Access from the R518 to the farm is via an unsurfaced secondary road of approximately 4 kilometres, connecting the property to Ga-Pila and adjacent rural areas.

The farm lies within a range of 20 kilometres, within a landscape shaped by both agricultural and mining activity, which directly influences environmental and operational conditions. Heavy vehicle movement along the R518 and surrounding routes contributes to dust, road degradation and heightened security exposure, particularly for slow-moving or lightly protected farm assets. Distance from Mokopane constrains rapid access to repair services, spare parts, law enforcement and emergency response, extending recovery times following incidents. Telecommunications coverage across the area is uneven, limiting coordination and real-time oversight of farm operations.

The broader setting is semi-arid, with dispersed land uses, low infrastructure density and long distances between service nodes. Although proximity to the R518 provides strategic connectivity in principle, the final access segment represents a persistent vulnerability. During periods of heavy rainfall, inadequate maintenance or security disruption, access reliability is compromised, affecting routine operations, emergency response and asset protection. These locational characteristics significantly influence the feasibility of effective farm management and the speed at which risks can be identified and addressed.

Security, Asset Protection and Human Safety Risk Assessment

From rom a security risk assessment perspective, Bellevue Farm presents a high-exposure, low-control risk environment that aligns precisely with what the Security-in-Depth (Defence in Depth) model defines as a structural security failure. This model, widely applied in intelligence, critical infrastructure protection and fiduciary risk oversight, is premised on a single core principle: effective security is achieved through multiple, mutually reinforcing layers, such that the failure of one layer is compensated for by others. At Bellevue Farm, this principle is not merely weakly applied; it is fundamentally absent.

When assessed against the standard layers of the Security-in-Depth (Defence in Depth) model, Bellevue Farm fails comprehensively across all five core elements: 

deterrence, 

detection, 

delay, 

response, and 

recovery. 

Each layer is either weak, fragmented or entirely absent, and critically, none of the layers compensate for the failure of another. 

Deterrence, the first and most visible layer, is weak. Vast areas of the property remain unfenced, human presence is predictable and frequently absent, there is no visible patrol dominance and no coherent perimeter or high-beam lighting strategy. These conditions do not discourage intrusion; they silently invite it. The size of the property, combined with prolonged periods of non-occupation across large sections, creates an open and permissive environment rather than a controlled one.

Detection capability exists only in fragments. CCTV coverage is present but intentionally undisclosed in number and uneven in distribution, resulting in extensive blind zones. While the cameras themselves are technically sophisticated, with night vision, motion detection, facial recognition, alarm triggers, automatic lighting activation and cloud-based recording, they are not integrated into any real-time response mechanism. There is no aerial surveillance, no intrusion analytics linked to intervention and no capacity to detect slow, incremental threats such as creeping land invasion or systematic asset stripping. Detection without response merely documents loss; it does not prevent it.

The delay layer, which should slow intruders long enough for intervention, is almost non-existent. Perimeter fencing has been breached repeatedly, including at least five times in the last 12 to 15 months on the Sepharane side of the Mogalakwena River. Large sections of the river boundary remain effectively unfenced and economically impractical to secure using conventional methods. There are no controlled access points, no buffer firebreaks functioning as physical barriers and no hardened zones protecting high-value assets. In risk terms, once intrusion occurs, there is nothing to stop progression.

Response capacity is functionally absent. The farm has no dedicated vehicle for emergencies or security intervention. Foot patrols remain the dominant mode of so-called security on a property of this scale, rendering them operationally irrelevant. Nine workers, including two supervisors and no farm manager, are dispersed across five housing points, creating security dilution rather than coverage. Distances between Portion 5 and Portion 2, and even between the workers’ compound and the lodge, are significant for a foot-dependent workforce supported by a single quad bike and two bicycles. In an emergency scenario, including snake bite, fire or violent intrusion, delayed response would almost certainly translate into loss of life.

Recovery, the final layer, is narrowly dependent on insurance. While the farm is insured, insurance is a financial instrument, not a life-saving mechanism. There is no redundancy planning for prolonged access cut-off, no continuity strategy for critical operations and no contingency for compound failures such as simultaneous security breach and extreme weather. Reliance on insurance in the absence of prevention reflects a compliance mindset rather than a risk management one.

The conclusion is unavoidable. Bellevue Farm does not possess layered security. It has isolated security artefacts, which in formal risk management doctrine is a recognised failure condition. This alone is sufficient to classify the farm as critically exposed. Security risk at Bellevue is not episodic or reactive; it is continuous, latent and compounding. The absence of visible crisis should not be mistaken for stability. In intelligence terms, this is a low-noise, high-consequence environment where threats incubate undetected until they manifest suddenly and irreversibly. Boards that fail in such contexts do so not through ignorance, but through dangerous comfort. Bellevue Farm is on a trajectory that demands urgent intervention, not reassurance.

Scale, Geography and the Illusion of Oversight

The most fundamental risk driver is scale. Bellevue Farm spans a large, 656-hectare footprint across Portions 2 and 5, with significant internal distances between housing, infrastructure, fields and boundaries. From a security doctrine standpoint, any asset that cannot be patrolled, monitored or responded to within minutes is already partially lost. Bellevue Farm fails this test comprehensively.

Foot patrols are functionally irrelevant on a property of this size. Yet, in practice, this remains the dominant mode of “security”. Nine workers, including two supervisors and no farm manager, are distributed across five housing points. This distribution creates security dilution rather than coverage. Each housing point becomes isolated instead of mutually reinforcing, while large sections of the farm experience extended periods of total human absence. The area beyond the Mogalakwena River on the Sepharane Village side is virtually non-patrolled, rendering it operationally ungoverned for significant stretches of time. Peripheral boundaries elsewhere on the farm are similarly exposed, with long intervals during which no human presence, observation or deterrence exists. In security risk terms, these conditions create predictable opportunity zones for theft, poaching, vandalism and gradual encroachment, with minimal likelihood of early detection or timely intervention.

Mobility Collapse and Emergency Fatality Risk

Mobility is the backbone of security. On Bellevue Farm, mobility is critically deficient. One quad bike and two bicycles serve an asset measured in hundreds of hectares. This is not a resource gap; it is a structural failure.

From an emergency response perspective, this alone places human life at unacceptable risk. There is no dedicated emergency vehicle. In practical terms, this means that a snake bite, severe burn, machinery injury or violent assault could easily result in death, not because treatment is unavailable, but because time to treatment exceeds survivability thresholds. This consititutes a foreseeable fatality condition. It does not require extraordinary circumstances. Ordinary incidents become lethal simply due to response incapacity. Trustees should understand clearly: continued operation under these conditions transfers moral and fiduciary liability directly to the custodial authority.

Surveillance Without Response: A Good Start but Still a Dangerous Half-Measure

CCTV surveillance exists and, at the time of reconnaissance, was reported to be operational and technically sophisticated. The system includes night vision, long-range capability, fixed and rotating cameras, motion and facial recognition, automatic alarm triggers, integrated lighting activation and cloud-based storage. These are strong features and should be acknowledged.

However, surveillance without response capability is an illusion of control. Detection that cannot be acted upon in real time does not prevent loss; it merely records it. The dotted nature of coverage leaves predictable blind spots, especially along some long sections of boundaries, remote infrastructure points and areas beyond the river.

The number and placement of cameras are intentionally undisclosed, which is good practice. What is not good practice is relying on cameras as a substitute for mobility, patrol capability and rapid escalation mechanisms. Cameras observe; they do not intervene. In high-exposure rural assets, surveillance must be paired with rapid response, layered deterrence and redundancy. Bellevue Farm has none of these in sufficient measure.

Perimeter Failure and the Imminent Risk of Land Loss

Repeated fence breaches, particularly on the Sepharane side, represent one of the most serious long-term threats to the Trust’s custodianship. Reports that fencing has been down for the fifth time within 12 to 15 months indicate chronic perimeter failure, not bad luck.

In security intelligence, persistent breaches are interpreted as probing behaviour. They test response, tolerance and enforcement thresholds. When breaches are repeatedly tolerated or slowly addressed, hostile actors escalate from opportunistic theft to de facto occupation.

The portion of land beyond the Mogalakwena River is especially vulnerable. It is difficult to access, expensive to secure and rarely monitored. This is precisely the type of terrain where slow, invisible land invasion occurs. Such encroachment does not begin with houses; it begins with grazing, temporary shelters, dumping and informal cultivation. By the time it is noticed, reversal becomes legally complex and politically volatile.

Without continuous monitoring, preferably through drone surveillance, the Trust is effectively blind to early-stage encroachment. Once occupation hardens, no amount of insurance, litigation or moral argument will restore the land easily.

Fire Risk: A Catastrophe Waiting on the Wings

Fire risk on Bellevue Farm is unmanaged to a degree that would be unacceptable in any commercial agricultural operation. Fireguards are poorly executed or absent. Firefighting equipment is absent. There are no structured fire response protocols.

The presence of a grass-thatched lodge dramatically amplifies this risk. While the lodge is powered by a reportedly efficient 3 kVA solar system, this creates a false sense of safety. Extreme weather events such as storms, prolonged cloud cover, lightning can disable solar systems for days. During such outages, the lodge becomes a highly flammable, poorly protected structure in an isolated environment.

The absence of fire suppression equipment near the lodge, combined with limited water access control and zero rapid evacuation capability, creates a scenario where loss of life is not hypothetical. It is only a matter of timing and conditions.

Alarms, Lighting and the Irresponsible Exposure of Critical Assets

None of the houses, storage facilities, sheds or meat processing and freezing areas are alarmed. There are no external high-beam security lights on buildings. This includes the container storeroom and shed complex, which contain movable, high-value assets and inputs. From a professional security standpoint, this configuration is indefensible. Cameras without alarms and lighting do not deter; they document. Containers and sheds without layered security are the first targets in rural asset stripping operations. The fact that a camera is nearby does not materially reduce risk if response capability is absent.

Human Factors: Awareness with Limited Capabilities

Two-way radios are present, which is a positive baseline measure. However, communication without mobility, escalation protocols or external linkages is functionally constrained and creates a false sense of control. Farm supervisors are not integrated into neighbouring farmer networks, rapid response structures or local mutual-aid arrangements, eliminating any prospect of timely reinforcement during incidents. There is no evidence of routine security briefings, drills, incident rehearsals or after-action reviews. As a result, communication exists in isolation, disconnected from response capability, authority or consequence.

Although the Implementing Agent has introduced certain systems, as reflected in available documentation, situational awareness among workers and effective security oversight by farm supervisors is effectively absent. Security awareness appears individual and experiential rather than institutionalised. This is inherently dangerous. Individuals compensate temporarily through habit and improvisation, but systems are what sustain control under stress. When individuals are fatigued, absent, intimidated or overwhelmed, awareness collapses abruptly, leaving assets and lives exposed.

Of particular concern is the high likelihood of internal collusive criminality, whether deliberate or through gross negligence. Unrecorded visitors have been observed on the property, tools and equipment are routinely left unsecured in open areas, and keys have reportedly been left on the quad bike. These behaviours are not benign. They indicate weak discipline, poor accountability and potential facilitation of later theft or sabotage. In security risk assessment, such patterns are classic precursors to insider-enabled crime, where external actors exploit internal lapses or tacit cooperation.

From a security risk assessment perspective, the most critical indicator of emerging threat on Bellevue Farm is system testing behaviour rather than any single offence. Adversaries rarely begin with high-value or high-visibility acts. They first probe for weakness: testing access points, observing response times, noting patterns of human absence, assessing lighting, mobility and communication gaps, and evaluating whether incursions trigger consequences. Where these probes encounter little resistance, escalation becomes rational rather than reckless.

In this context, the confirmed presence of poaching snares must be read as an early-stage diagnostic signal, not the core problem itself. Poaching is merely one low-risk manifestation of system testing. It indicates repeated unauthorised entry, growing familiarity with terrain and confidence that detection is unlikely. This reflects successful reconnaissance. Where such probing is neither detected nor disrupted, it typically graduates into theft, infrastructure sabotage, organised trespass and, ultimately, land encroachment. The danger lies not in the act observed, but in what it reveals: a permissive environment in which threat actors can learn, adapt and advance without interruption.

Final Professional Assessment

Bellevue Farm is not secure. It is critically exposed. The current operating environment reflects a condition in which risk is not being managed, mitigated or controlled, but rather absorbed by chance. The Trust is relying on luck, goodwill and the absence of catastrophe as substitutes for structured security governance. This is not risk management; it is risk deferral.

It does not prevent death, does not reverse land invasion, does not restore institutional credibility and does not shield Trustees from moral, fiduciary or reputational accountability. In environments where human life, community-owned land and long-term restitution assets are at stake, insurance is the last layer of defence, not the first.

From a senior security risk assessment perspective, the most alarming feature is not any single deficiency documented in this section, but the normalisation of systemic exposure. This is the point at which governance failure typically begins. When decision-makers become comfortable operating inside a high-risk envelope, warning signs are reinterpreted as inconveniences, and absence of incident is mistaken for safety. In intelligence doctrine, this condition precedes sudden, irreversible loss.

Bellevue Farm exhibits all the indicators of a low-noise, high-consequence risk environment. Threats are incubating quietly rather than manifesting explosively. System testing is already occurring. Perimeter probing has been confirmed. Poaching, fence breaches, internal discipline lapses and prolonged unmonitored zones are not isolated problems; they are signals. They indicate that hostile actors are learning the terrain, understanding response limitations and gaining confidence that escalation will not be met with timely resistance.

Critically, the Trust currently holds custodianship of an asset that is geographically expansive, operationally complex and symbolically significant, yet is defended by a security posture that would be inadequate even for a fraction of its scale. The absence of layered deterrence, rapid mobility, integrated response and institutionalised security discipline creates a foreseeable pathway to outcomes that will be framed retrospectively as “unexpected”, but which are, in fact, predictable.

This section is not a commentary or criticism. It is a formal warning. The question before the Board of Trustees is no longer whether Bellevue Farm faces serious security and human safety risk. That question has been answered unequivocally by the evidence. The only remaining question is how long inaction can be sustained before the Trust is confronted with consequences that cannot be undone.

Hazard, Risk and Vulnerability Profile

 Introduction

Disaster risk in Ga-Pila is shaped by persistent, localised hazards that affect households and community systems on a recurring basis rather than through rare, large-scale disaster events. The dominant risks are seasonal and every day in nature, arising from predictable conditions such as flooding linked to inadequate stormwater management, wind damage to housing, household fires associated with unsafe energy use, public health outbreaks linked to sanitation and waste management constraints, and access disruptions caused by deteriorating internal roads. These risks do not occur in isolation; they recur annually and accumulate over time, steadily eroding household assets, livelihoods and social resilience.

This risk profile is consistent with the Disaster Management Act (57 of 2002), the National Disaster Management Framework and the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030), all of which emphasise the importance of understanding risk as a product of hazard exposure, vulnerability and underlying development conditions. The analysis presented in this section integrates community perspectives, observed settlement conditions and technical assessment to identify both current and emerging disaster risks affecting Ga-Pila. It recognises that vulnerability is shaped as much by service reliability, infrastructure quality and environmental exposure as by the hazards themselves.

During community engagements conducted in July 2024, one resident captured the lived reality of this cumulative risk with stark clarity:

“When the rains come or a fire breaks out, we already count what we will lose. Not because we are careless, but because nothing ever changes. We prepare ourselves for damage, not for help.”

This statement reflects a pattern of normalised exposure rather than episodic crisis. In Ga-Pila, disaster risk is not experienced as an exceptional interruption, but as a constant background condition that households anticipate and absorb. Such conditions reveal the interaction between environmental hazards and long-standing development constraints, including uneven service provision, ageing infrastructure and limited household coping capacity. Without addressing these root causes, risk reduction efforts will remain reactive and insufficient, allowing avoidable losses to continue accumulating quietly at community level.

 Risk Analysis Methodology

South Africa’s National Disaster Management Framework (NDMF, 2005), in alignment with international best practice including the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030) and guidance from UNDRR, IFRC and the World Bank’s Global Facility for Disaster Reduction and Recovery, adopts a risk-based and prevention-oriented approach to disaster management. This framework prioritises systematic risk understanding, vulnerability reduction, resilience building, and the strengthening of preparedness and response capabilities.

At its core, disaster risk is conceptualised as the interaction between hazards, underlying vulnerability and existing capacity, shaped by the level of exposure:

Disaster Risk = Hazard × Vulnerability × Exposure ÷ Capacity

Where:

Hazard

:

Potential damaging physical event (e.g., floods, drought, disease outbreaks, hazardous materials)

Vulnerability

:

Conditions that increase susceptibility to harm (e.g. poverty, weak infrastructure, poor planning)

Exposure

:

People, assets, services or systems located in hazard-prone areas

Capacity

:

Capabilities and resources to prevent, withstand, and recover (e.g. early warning, emergency services, governance)

This logical sequence drives both the NDMF and global DRR practice:

Know Your Risk: Hazard, exposure, and vulnerability profiling

Prevent and Mitigate: Reduce risk drivers, build resilience, apply land use control, enforce standards

Prepare: Plans, systems, early warning, simulations, readiness

Respond: Coordinated, rapid interventions to save lives and protect livelihoods

Recover and Build Back Better: Restore and improve systems to reduce future risk

This approach reflects the broader risk management cycle and is designed to support development continuity rather than reactive crisis management. The disaster risk logic underpinning the NDMF and international best practice is deliberately proactive, risk-informed, development-oriented and resilience-focused. It rejects the notion of disasters as unavoidable acts of fate and instead treats them as governance and planning challenges that can be anticipated, mitigated and systematically managed. Against this backdrop, the assessment methodology integrated field-based evidence, community perceptions of risk, spatial observation and institutional review. Exposure Analysis

Hazards Exposure refers to the presence of people, assets, livelihoods and systems in locations that could be adversely affected by hazards. Exposure does not in itself create disaster risk; rather, it determines who and what is placed in harm’s way when hazards interact with vulnerability conditions. Building on the hazard identification in Section 3.3, this section analyses exposure patterns separately for the Ga-Pila village settlement and Bellevue Farm, recognising their fundamentally different spatial forms, functions and risk dynamics.

This approach is consistent with the disaster risk logic embedded in the National Disaster Management Framework and Sendai Framework Priority 1, which require that risk analysis distinguish between hazard presence and exposure characteristics in order to inform targeted risk reduction.

The dual exposure profiles of the Ga-Pila village and Bellevue Farm confirm that disaster risk in the study area is spatially differentiated and functionally distinct. Village risk is driven by population density and service fragility, while farm risk is driven by scale, asset exposure and governance gaps. Section 3.6 (Vulnerability Analysis) follows by examining the social, economic, institutional and infrastructural conditions that explain why these exposed people and assets experience harm when hazards occur. This ensures that risk reduction and response planning are grounded not only in where hazards strike, but in why their impacts become severe and persistent.

 Vulnerability Analysis

Vulnerability refers to the conditions that increase the susceptibility of people, livelihoods, infrastructure and systems to harm when hazards occur. While exposure identifies who or what is at risk, vulnerability explains why loss, disruption and delayed recovery occur, and why the same hazards repeatedly produce disproportionate consequences for certain groups or assets. Vulnerability is shaped by physical fragility, economic insecurity, social inequality, environmental degradation and institutional limitations, all of which influence the ability to anticipate, absorb, respond to and recover from hazard impacts.

The National Disaster Management Framework emphasises that disaster risk in South Africa is fundamentally a development challenge rather than a natural one. Disasters are rarely the result of hazards alone; they emerge where hazards intersect with long-standing vulnerability conditions created by poverty, inadequate planning, weak infrastructure and limited access to services. This logic applies to both the Ga-Pila village settlement and Bellevue Farm, although the nature and expression of vulnerability differ significantly between the two contexts. Accordingly, vulnerability analysis is presented separately for the Ga-Pila village and Bellevue Farm.

Vulnerability Analysis: Ga-Pila Village

In the Ga-Pila village, vulnerability is primarily human-centred and socially embedded. It arises from a combination of household poverty, fragile housing conditions, limited livelihood security, constrained access to services and weak institutional support. These conditions mean that even low- to medium-intensity hazards produce significant hardship and long recovery periods. The key vulnerability conditions affecting the Ga-Pila village are summarised in Table 3.5A.

Vulnerability within the village is unevenly distributed. Groups that consistently experience heightened vulnerability include children, elderly residents, people with disabilities, female-headed households and households without stable income. These groups often lack durable housing, savings, private transport, secure sanitation and rapid access to assistance. As a result, they are less able to prepare for hazard events, protect assets or recover quickly after loss.

Within the elderly population, particular attention must be paid to surviving residents who personally experienced relocation from Old Ga-Pila. Unlike younger generations, they often have reduced capacity to rebuild after loss or adapt to repeated shocks. A major driver of vulnerability in the village is weak physical resilience at household level. Many dwellings lack reinforced roofing, proper foundations or safe electrical installations, increasing susceptibility to wind damage, flooding and fire. Informal backyard rooms and ageing structures are particularly exposed. When damage occurs, households with limited income or physical capacity struggle to carry out repairs, resulting in repeated exposure to subsequent events.

Systemic vulnerability is further intensified by fragile essential services. Dependence on a single borehole and constrained electricity infrastructure means that service failures affect large portions of the settlement simultaneously. While all households are impacted, consequences are more severe for those with limited mobility, poor health or no alternative coping mechanisms. Limited access to emergency transport and slow response times exacerbates these conditions during medical emergencies or fires.

The farm’s large size, dispersed infrastructure and long, poorly monitored boundaries create inherent vulnerability to environmental hazards, security threats and slow-onset losses. Limited mobility, absence of rapid response capacity and reliance on foot patrols mean that hazards are often detected late and addressed slowly. In both contexts, vulnerability cannot be addressed through emergency response alone. It requires corrective development planning, infrastructure strengthening, governance reform and targeted protection of high-risk groups and assets. Failure to address these root conditions ensures that hazards will continue to translate into loss, disruption and long-term erosion of resilience.

Section 3.7 follows by examining existing capacity and coping mechanisms, identifying the strengths, resources and social systems that can be mobilised to offset vulnerability and support more effective, equitable and sustainable disaster risk reduction across both the village and Bellevue Farm.

 Capacity and Coping Mechanisms

While Section 3.5 examined the vulnerability conditions that intensify disaster risk in Ga-Pila, this section focuses on the capacities, coping mechanisms and resilience assets that enable households, institutions and operational systems to withstand, respond to and recover from hazard impacts. In disaster risk management terms, capacity refers to the knowledge, skills, resources, organisational arrangements and social networks that reduce risk and moderate the consequences of hazard events.

Capacity across the Ga-Pila area is uneven and context-specific. In the village, capacity is primarily social and adaptive, rooted in community cohesion and informal systems. At Bellevue Farm, capacity is operational and asset-based, but fragmented and insufficient relative to the scale and exposure of the asset. These two capacity environments are therefore assessed separately.

Capacity and Coping Mechanisms (Ga-Pila Village)

Capacity in the Ga-Pila village exists largely at household and community level and is predominantly informal, socially embedded and experience-driven rather than institutionally resourced. While these capacities do not eliminate risk or compensate for structural development deficits, they play a critical buffering role, particularly in the absence of rapid external emergency response.

Community capacity in Ga-Pila is anchored in strong social cohesion and shared responsibility. Residents routinely assist one another during hazard events such as flooding, household fires, illness and access disruption. Common coping actions include diverting stormwater away from homesteads, reinforcing roofs against wind damage, pooling labour to clear access paths after storms, sharing food during service interruptions and assisting vulnerable neighbours during crises. These responses are not formally organised, but they are deeply embedded in everyday social practice.

Long-standing social institutions are central to sustaining resilience. Churches, burial societies, stokvels and informal savings groups provide financial, emotional and logistical support during periods of loss and stress. Although not designed as disaster response mechanisms, these structures significantly reduce social fragmentation and hardship following hazard events. Their contribution is particularly important for low-income households, elderly residents and those with limited physical capacity.

Local and indigenous knowledge also contributes meaningfully to preparedness. Community members, especially older residents, possess detailed understanding of seasonal patterns, flood-prone zones, safe movement routes and environmental signals associated with drought or heavy rainfall. While this knowledge is not systematically documented or formally integrated into planning processes, it informs household-level decisions such as relocating livestock, securing property and adjusting daily activities during high-risk periods.

A notable strengthening of organised local capacity occurred in 2025 with the establishment of the Ga-Pila Disaster Management Volunteer Force (DMVF). The DMVF consists of twenty community members who completed a twelve-week Basic Disaster Risk Management First Responder Programme. Volunteers provide immediate response to household fires, storm incidents, missing-person searches and community safety alerts, and support public awareness activities. This represents a significant step towards institutionalising local disaster response capacity, even though the team currently operates without dedicated equipment, transport or formal integration into municipal disaster management structures.

Institutional capacity within the village remains limited but provides important entry points for strengthening resilience. Traditional leadership structures retain legitimacy and play a stabilising role during crises by mobilising community participation and mediating disputes. Ward committee structures, school governance bodies and water committees contribute to coordination and information flow, particularly in managing borehole access and resolving water-related conflict during shortages. However, these structures have limited authority over infrastructure protection and service delivery and lack access to dedicated disaster resources.

Capacity and Coping Mechanisms (Bellevue Farm)

Capacity at Bellevue Farm differs fundamentally from that of the village. It is asset-centric, operational and spatially distributed, but critically misaligned with the scale, exposure and risk profile of the farm. While some capacity elements exist, they are fragmented, under-resourced and insufficient to manage foreseeable hazards or emergencies. The principal capacity elements and limitations at Bellevue Farm are summarised in Table 3.6B.

The farm benefits from limited operational assets, including basic on-site housing, two-way radios for communication, a quad bike and bicycles for movement, and a dotted CCTV system with advanced technical features. These elements provide a baseline level of operational awareness and communication. However, they do not constitute a coherent or integrated capacity system. Human capacity on the farm is thinly spread. Nine workers, including two supervisors and no farm manager, are distributed across multiple residential points on a property exceeding 600 hectares. This limits the ability to concentrate response, sustain patrols or mount coordinated interventions. Capacity is therefore highly vulnerable to absence, fatigue and concurrent incidents.

Emergency response capacity on the farm is particularly weak. There is no dedicated emergency vehicle, no on-site firefighting equipment, no alarm systems on buildings, no external high-beam lighting and no designated evacuation or assembly points. While two-way radios enable communication, communication without mobility, escalation protocols or external linkages has limited practical value in emergencies. Institutional and network capacity is also constrained. Farm supervisors are not formally linked to neighbouring farmers, rapid response groups or mutual-aid arrangements, limiting the possibility of reinforcement during incidents. There is no documented emergency response plan, no routine drills and no redundancy planning for compound failures such as extreme weather combined with security breaches. In effect, Bellevue Farm possesses isolated capacity artefacts rather than an integrated capacity system. These artefacts can delay loss marginally, but they cannot prevent escalation or protect life under adverse conditions.

This confirms that disaster risk reduction cannot rely on existing coping mechanisms alone. Capacity must be strengthened, integrated and scaled to match exposure and vulnerability. Section 3.8 follows by integrating hazard, vulnerability and capacity findings to prioritise disaster risks, providing the basis for targeted risk reduction measures and strategic interventions proposed in Chapter 4.

 Risk Prioritisation

Risk prioritisation integrates the findings of the hazard analysis (Section 3.4), vulnerability analysis (Section 3.5) and capacity and coping assessment (Section 3.6) to determine which disaster risks require immediate and sustained intervention. Not all hazards pose the same level of threat. Some occur frequently and generate cumulative losses, while others occur less often but carry the potential for severe consequences, including loss of life, irreversible asset damage or systemic failure.

This section applies a structured and evidence-based approach to distinguish between priority risks and lower-order risks, thereby providing a defensible basis for sequencing disaster risk reduction actions and allocating limited resources where preventative impact will be greatest.

Risk prioritisation follows the disaster risk logic applied in South Africa’s National Disaster Management Framework and the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction, which define disaster risk as a function of hazard likelihood, exposure, vulnerability and capacity. In this assessment, risk was calculated using the following relationship:

Risk = Hazard × Vulnerability × Exposure ÷ Capacity

Each identified hazard was scored on a scale of one to five for likelihood, impact, vulnerability and exposure. Capacity was scored inversely, recognising that stronger capacity reduces overall risk. The resulting risk scores were grouped into four tiers using the classification framework set out in Table 3.7.

Risk Prioritisation (Ga-Pila Village

The risk profile for Ga-Pila Village confirms that disaster risk is driven primarily by systemic service vulnerabilities and high-frequency settlement-level hazards, rather than rare catastrophic events. Risk manifests as persistent stress on households, infrastructure and social systems, with cumulative impacts that steadily erode resilience.

Tier 1 risks represent conditions that pose immediate and serious threats to life, health and community stability. These include water supply interruption, flooding and stormwater runoff, household and structural fires, unsafe electrical wiring and drought-related water stress. These hazards affect large portions of the population simultaneously and have the potential to escalate rapidly into humanitarian crises if unmanaged.

Tier 2 risks consist of serious recurring hazards that compound vulnerability over time. These include electricity disruptions, strong winds, road access failure, veld and grass fires, waterborne disease and animal disease outbreaks. While not always immediately fatal, they undermine livelihoods, disrupt essential services and weaken household recovery capacity.

Tier 3 risks are emerging or moderate risks linked to environmental degradation, climate stress, health pressures and socio-economic strain. Individually manageable, their cumulative effect contributes to long-term vulnerability.

Tier 4 risks, such as lightning and tailings dam failure, have lower likelihood but potentially severe consequences and therefore require monitoring, preparedness and early warning integration rather than routine mitigation investment.

Risk Prioritisation – Bellevue Farm

The risk profile for Bellevue Farm differs fundamentally from that of the village. While population exposure is lower, the consequences of failure are significantly higher, and capacity to respond independently is weaker. Disaster risk on the farm is therefore characterised by low-frequency but high-consequence events that threaten operational continuity, asset integrity and human safety.

Tier 1 risks on the farm are dominated by existential and systemic threats, including water supply failure, fire incidents, access failure during emergencies, security breaches, land encroachment and severe drought. In several cases, limited on-site response capacity and distance from emergency services amplify risk despite lower exposure.

Tier 2 risks include electricity system failure, veld fires, occupational injuries and extreme weather exposure. These hazards disrupt operations, increase liability and escalate rapidly if not contained.

Tier 3 and Tier 4 risks are largely environmental or technical in nature and require monitoring, maintenance and preparedness rather than intensive mitigation investment.

Integrated Disaster Risk Management Plan; Ga-Pila (Sterkwater) Village

Strategic Posture and Operational Context

Disaster risk in Ga-Pila Village is not episodic, exceptional or confined to extreme events. It is a persistent and cumulative condition produced by everyday development realities, fragile service systems, settlement patterns, environmental stress and limited institutional reach. The risk profile established in Chapters 1 to 3 confirms that the dominant threats to life, livelihoods and community stability arise from recurrent and interacting hazards such as water supply interruptions, flooding and stormwater runoff, unsafe energy use, household fires, health risks and access failure, rather than from rare catastrophic events alone.

This Integrated Disaster Risk Management Framework adopts a proactive, prevention-led and community-centred strategic posture. It deliberately shifts Ga-Pila away from a reactive disaster response model towards a structured system that reduces risk at source, strengthens preparedness, enables disciplined response and supports recovery in a manner that stabilises development gains. Disaster risk management is therefore treated as a core development function, not an auxiliary emergency activity.

Operationally, the framework recognises the realities that shape Ga-Pila’s risk environment. Emergency services are distant, response times are constrained, and first response will almost always be local. Community capacity exists, but it is uneven, largely informal and under-resourced. Essential services such as water, electricity and access routes are single-point dependent and prone to cascading failure. Vulnerability is socially differentiated, with children, elderly residents, people with disabilities and low-income households experiencing disproportionate impact during hazard events.

Within this context, the framework establishes a locally anchored, institutionally aligned and operationally disciplined system for managing disaster risk. It integrates prevention, preparedness, response and recovery into a single coherent logic, anchored in community leadership and progressively linked to municipal and district disaster management structures. The framework provides the strategic foundation upon which the Operational Framework in Section 4.3 is built.

The Strategic Framework defines the intent, direction and organising logic of disaster risk management in Ga-Pila Village. It articulates the outcomes the community seeks to achieve, the principles that guide decision-making, and the enabling conditions required to translate intent into sustained action. The framework is informed by the risk analysis presented in Chapter 3 and aligned with the Disaster Management Act (Act 57 of 2002), the National Disaster Management Framework and the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction.

A safe, prepared and resilient Ga-Pila Village where lives, livelihoods, services and community assets are protected from disaster risk, and where development progress is not repeatedly undermined by avoidable hazards.

Mission

To reduce disaster risk in Ga-Pila Village through proactive prevention, targeted risk mitigation, strengthened local capacity and coordinated risk governance, ensuring that disaster risk management supports sustainable development and community wellbeing.

Strategic Objectives

To reduce priority disaster risks that threaten human life, health, livelihoods and community stability in Ga-Pila Village.

To protect critical services, including water supply, energy provision, road access and public health systems, from recurrent disruption and cascading failure.

To promote safer settlement practices, infrastructure resilience and environmental protection as a means of addressing structural vulnerability.

To strengthen community-based and institutional capacity for disaster risk management, preparedness, response and recovery.

To enable coordinated governance, partnerships and resource mobilisation for sustained disaster risk reduction.

Strategic Pillars

The framework is structured around five mutually reinforcing strategic pillars:

Protection of Essential Services: Safeguarding water, energy, access routes and health systems against disruption that rapidly escalates into disaster conditions.

Community Preparedness and Resilience: Strengthening local readiness, first response capability and collective action before external support arrives.

Flooding and stormwater runoff within the settlement

Unsafe electrical connections and energy practices

Drought and prolonged water scarcity

These risks represent the highest leverage points for reducing loss, disruption and repeated hardship in Ga-Pila Village.

Strategic Principles

Implementation of this framework is governed by the following principles:

Risk reduction before response: Preventing and reducing risk takes precedence over managing consequences.

Community-centred resilience: Local capacity is the foundation of effective disaster risk management.

Prevention through development: Disaster risk reduction must be integrated into everyday planning and service delivery.

Shared responsibility: Disaster risk management is a collective function across households, community structures and institutions.

Local capability strengthening: External support must reinforce, not replace, local systems.

Transparency and accountability: Clear authority, communication and documentation underpin trust and effectiveness.

Sustainability and continuity: Actions must be maintainable beyond individual events or funding cycles.

Improving local risk information, hazard monitoring and early warning mechanisms.

Building structured working relationships with Mogalakwena Local Municipality and the Waterberg District Disaster Management Centre.

Embedding disaster risk considerations into local development planning, service management and investment decisions.

Disaster Risk Management Plan

 Introduction and Purpose

This Bellevue Farm represents a disaster risk environment that is fundamentally distinct from that of Ga-Pila Village. Whereas village risk is dominated by household vulnerability, settlement density and service fragility, disaster risk on Bellevue Farm is shaped by exposure of productive assets, dependence on natural systems, spatial dispersion, and the need for uninterrupted agricultural operations.

The Farm functions simultaneously as:

A productive economic enterprise;

A workplace with occupational health and safety obligations;

A land-based ecological system;

A repository of high-value fixed and mobile assets; and

A livelihood platform linked to broader community benefit.

Disaster risk in this context is therefore not episodic. It is embedded within production cycles, environmental conditions and operational systems. Even moderate hazard events can generate disproportionate economic loss, asset degradation and long-term productivity decline if not actively managed.

The purpose of this chapter is to establish a farm-specific, integrated disaster risk management framework and operational plan that:

Protects human life, livestock and agricultural assets;

Reduces vulnerability of land, water and production systems;

Ensures continuity of operations during and after hazard events;

Limits cascading and secondary losses; and

Embeds disaster risk management into routine farm governance and decision-making.

This chapter translates the risk analysis presented in Chapter 3 into operationally enforceable measures, aligned with agricultural best practice, the National Disaster Management Framework and the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction.

Strategic Risk Posture for Bellevue Farm

Distinctive Farm Risk Characteristics

The disaster risk posture of Bellevue Farm is characterised by:

Concentration of productive assets, including boreholes, irrigation infrastructure, fencing, buildings, equipment and livestock;

Direct dependence on climatic and environmental conditions, with limited buffering capacity during droughts, floods and extreme weather;

Seasonal labour and activity cycles, which influence supervision, safety and response capacity;

Biosecurity exposure, particularly relating to animal disease and contamination risks;

Spatial dispersion of infrastructure, increasing detection and response times; and

Cascading risk dynamics, where failure in one subsystem rapidly triggers secondary losses across production, access and security.

As a result, disaster risk on the Farm is systemic, cumulative and operationally consequential. Risk management failure does not only threaten safety; it undermines economic viability and long-term sustainability.

Strategic Framework

Strategic Posture

The strategic posture for Bellevue Farm is one of risk containment, production continuity and asset preservation. Disaster risk management is treated as a core operational discipline, not an emergency add-on or compliance exercise.

The framework adopts a preventive, anticipatory and continuity-oriented posture, recognising that:

Prevention is significantly more cost-effective than recovery;

Delayed response amplifies losses exponentially in agricultural systems; and

Farm resilience is achieved through disciplined systems rather than reactive intervention.

Vision

A resilient, productive and secure Bellevue Farm where agricultural operations, livelihoods and assets are protected from disaster risk.

Mission

To reduce disaster risk on Bellevue Farm through proactive prevention, operational preparedness, disciplined response and structured recovery that safeguard production continuity and long-term sustainability.

Strategic Objectives

The disaster risk management framework for Bellevue Farm is guided by the following objectives:

Protect farm workers, residents and contractors from hazard exposure.

Safeguard critical agricultural infrastructure and production assets.

Reduce vulnerability of land, livestock and water resources.

Ensure continuity of farming operations during hazard events.

Enable rapid stabilisation and recovery following disruptions.

Embed disaster risk reduction into everyday farm management practice.

Strategic Principles

Implementation of this framework is governed by the following principles:

Asset protection before loss compensation;

Prevention embedded in routine operations;

Production continuity as a risk reduction objective;

Clear operational accountability and authority;

Environmental stewardship as a resilience measure; and

Build-back-better as a recovery standard.

Institutional and Community Coordination Arrangements 

Purpose and Scope

This chapter establishes the institutional, governance, coordination, and accountability arrangements that underpin disaster risk management for Ga-Pila (Sterkwater) Village and Bellevue Farm. While both spatial areas fall under a single Disaster Risk Management Plan, they represent fundamentally different risk environments and therefore require distinct institutional configurations, command arrangements, and coordination systems.

The purpose of this chapter is to ensure that disaster risk management is authoritative, coordinated, and enforceable, with no ambiguity regarding leadership, operational responsibility, or escalation pathways. The chapter applies across the full disaster management continuum, namely, prevention, preparedness, response, recovery, and continuity, and provides the institutional backbone that enables the effective implementation of Chapters 4 and 5.

This chapter is applicable to:

Community-based disaster risk management within Ga-Pila Village;

Asset, production, and environmental risk management on Bellevue Farm; and

All internal and external stakeholders involved in disaster risk reduction and disaster response within these two spatial systems.

Governance Logic and Authority Separation

Disaster risk governance within this Plan is guided by the principle that authority must follow risk context. Although Ga-Pila Village and Bellevue Farm are geographically linked and share certain support structures, they differ significantly in:

Population density and vulnerability profiles;

Asset concentration and economic exposure;

Nature of hazards and escalation dynamics; and

Operational response requirements.

For this reason, a single, unified institutional arrangement would weaken accountability and compromise response effectiveness. Instead, this Plan adopts a dual-system governance logic, whereby:

Ga-Pila Village operates under a community-centred disaster governance model, prioritising life safety, public health, household protection, and social stability; and

Bellevue Farm operates under an asset- and production-centred disaster governance model, prioritising occupational safety, fire control, environmental protection, and business continuity.

Despite this separation, both systems are anchored within a common legal, policy, and oversight framework, ensuring coherence, coordination, and escalation compatibility when required.

Disaster risk management in Ga-Pila Village is led through a community-based governance model, recognising that hazards in the village environment affect households, public services, and vulnerable populations simultaneously. Annexure 5: Early Warning and Emergency Communication Protocol

Referenced in Section 5.4 - Preparedness and Early Warning and Section 6.4 - Coordination

Purpose

To provide a systematic and community-integrated protocol for detecting, validating, communicating, and acting on early warning information in Ga-Pila. The protocol ensures that risk information translates into timely, inclusive, and actionable communication that saves lives, safeguards livelihoods, and protects local development gains.

This annexure operationalises the Sendai Framework Priority 4, Enhancing Disaster Preparedness for Effective Response and to “Build Back Better” in Recovery, Rehabilitation and Reconstruction, and aligns with Key Performance Area 4 of South Africa’s National Disaster Management Framework (NDMF, 2005).

5.1 Conceptual Basis

Early warning is not an event but a systemic capability built upon science, communication, trust, and social mobilisation. Its effectiveness depends on four interdependent pillars:

Risk Knowledge: Understanding who and what is at risk;

Monitoring and Forecasting: Continuous detection and interpretation of hazard data;

Dissemination and Communication: Reliable, accessible, and trusted message delivery;

Response Capability: The ability and willingness of people to act when warned.

In the Ga-Pila context, this framework integrates scientific observation (meteorological and hydrological), indigenous knowledge, and community reporting through a decentralised system led by the Ga-Pila Community Development Trust (GCDT) and implemented by the Disaster Management Volunteer Force (DMVF).

5.2 Principles Guiding the Protocol

The protocol is anchored in the following principles, distilled from decades of field learning and global best practice:

Speed and Accuracy: The first message must be the right message — timely, verified, and actionable.

Trust and Credibility: Communities act when they trust the source; hence, all warnings are authenticated through established structures.

Inclusivity and Reach: No household or vulnerable group should be left uninformed or unable to respond.

Two-Way Communication: Early warning is as much about receiving local signals as it is about sending official alerts.

Redundancy and Resilience: Multiple communication pathways ensure continuity during power or network failures.

5.5 Communication Channels and Protocols

The Ga-Pila system adopts a multi-channel, multi-institutional approach, drawing from field-tested communication redundancy principles:

Primary Channels:

GCDT-verified WhatsApp broadcast lists (community-wide, bilingual).

Direct liaison with Traditional Council, Ward Committee, School Principals, and Clinic Head.

Local faith-based and cultural institutions.

Door-to-door messengers for elderly or disabled persons.

Local radio (where possible) for wide-area alerts.

6.1 Conceptual Foundation

Incident response requires a balance between structure and flexibility. Drawing from more than three decades of operational experience in Africa, it is evident that community-based response functions best when:

Roles and lines of authority are clearly defined and widely understood.

Decisions are taken as close as possible to the point of impact.

Communication flows are short, direct, and verifiable.

The Ga-Pila framework therefore follows a three-tier activation structure:

Level 1 (Local Containment): Incidents manageable within the community using local resources.

Level 2 (Supported Response): Incidents that exceed community capacity and require municipal or sectoral assistance.

Level 3 (Escalated Disaster): Incidents beyond local and municipal capacity that require district or provincial coordination.

This model is consistent with the National Disaster Management Framework’s emphasis on decentralised but coordinated response capability.

Annexure 11: Minimum Equipment List

Referenced in Section 5.4 (Preparedness and Early Warning), and Section 6.4 (Institutional Structure)

Purpose

This annexure outlines the minimum equipment standards required for the effective operation of the Disaster Risk Reduction and Response Task Team (DRRRTT) in Ga-Pila. It ensures that the community has the tools necessary for preparedness, early response, and recovery, while integrating mechanisms for long-term sustainability through community-based management and limited commercial use of assets.

The standards are informed by Sphere (2018), NDMC (2017), and Sendai Framework (2015–2030) guidelines, contextualised for a rural community of 2,028 residents (487 households).

Guiding Principles

Adequacy: Equipment levels match risk exposure and population size.

Safety: All tools must comply with minimum safety and quality standards.

Simplicity: Equipment must be locally operable and serviceable.

Durability: Assets must be built for rugged, field conditions.

Sustainability: Equipment management must allow for self-financing and continuity through practical use and community stewardship.

Management, Maintenance, and Deployment

Custodianship: Managed by the GCDT Disaster Desk, with DMVF oversight.

Storage: Under cover at the Community Hall and Study Centre; secured and kept filled at 70–80% capacity to maintain pump integrity.

Maintenance: Monthly pressure checks, hose inspections, and trailer servicing recorded in the DMVF Equipment Register.

Revenue Use: Hire income from bowsers to fund maintenance, tyre replacement, pump servicing, and repainting.

Training: Refresher courses every six months for all DMVF and security staff handling the bowsers.

Procurement follows GCDT Procurement Policy and relevant safety standards.

DMVF Safety Officer maintains a Procurement and Maintenance Register (serials, warranties, inspections).

Annual joint audit by the GCDT, DMVF Secretariat, and Municipal DMC.

Financial tracking of bowser income and expenditure ensures transparency and accountability.
